# Part 3 Summary — Do Companies' Words and Disclosures Line Up?

Parts 1 and 2 give every company two profiles: the values it
advertises on its About page, and the values it emphasises in its
formal proxy disclosures, scored on the same nine themes with the
same instrument. This part asks whether the two profiles point the
same way. The answer is a number per company per year, an
"authenticity index", built to reward relative consistency: a
company scores high when the themes it stresses *more than other
companies do* in its advertising are the same themes it stresses
more than other companies do in its disclosures. Scores run from
-1 (the two profiles pull in opposite directions) to +1 (perfect
relative alignment); the mechanics (standardising each theme within
its document type, and matching each year's About page to the
following spring's proxy, which reports on that year) are described
in the README.

Across 121 company-years the index averages 0.16 with wide spread
(standard deviation 0.31; range -0.65 to 0.84) — firms genuinely
differ, and the same firm moves over time. The average firm became
more aligned into 2021 (mean 0.25), with a modest
dip in 2022. That peak is not an artefact of everyone
talking about the same things during a crisis: when each year is
standardised internally, removing the common shock, the 2021 peak
is still there, slightly stronger.

At the top of the ranking sit Broadcom (0.55), Amazon (0.47) and
JPMorgan (0.40); at the bottom, Microsoft (-0.25), with most other
low scores bunched near zero. Two cautions belong next to any such
list. First, this measures *disclosure alignment, not corporate
ethics*: a low score typically reflects a structural mismatch,
an About page and a proxy that naturally talk about different
things, not a finding of dishonesty. Second, two of the most extreme firms
(Broadcom at the top, Microsoft at the bottom) have fiscal years
that end off-cycle, so the year-matching convention may misalign
them by a year; their positions carry that asterisk. For
Microsoft the asterisk explains nothing away: under the timing
that fits its fiscal calendar (same-year: the October proxy
reports on the fiscal year the July snapshot falls in), its score
is -0.40, and it ranks lowest under either convention.

The validity check planned in advance behaves as it should. Wells Fargo,
whose fake-accounts scandal broke in September 2016, scores well
below average over 2016–19 (mean 0.06 against a sample mean of
0.16, seventh-lowest of 24 firms) and turns negative by 2019; the
year-to-year path is noisy, and its 2016 reading, taken from a
page archived that July, *before* the scandal broke, sits near
the sample average. An index that flattered Wells Fargo in those
years would be hard to trust; this one does not.

What the index cannot do is see behaviour itself: both inputs are
things companies wrote. It measures whether the story told to
customers and the story told to shareholders are the same story —
which is why Part 4 turns from levels to response: when an outside
shock arrives, which of the two stories actually changes?

Used carefully, the index's value is comparative: it shows which firms' two stories track each other and which pull apart, and a score is a prompt for questions, not a verdict on any firm.
